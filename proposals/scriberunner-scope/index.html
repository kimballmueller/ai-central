02

The short version

If you read nothing else, read this.

THE PROBLEM

You already do this. It just isn't a system.

Ria finds these facilities today — reads the surveys, spots the citations, writes the email. She is good at it, which is the entire reason this can be built at all. The problem is that it runs on her hands, and she also runs the clinical department. One person researching one facility at a time is not a pipeline. It is a bottleneck with a very capable person stuck inside it.

THE FIX

A system that qualifies leads out of any source you point it at.

Star Pro, the public government records, a purchased list, your own past clients — it pulls from any source of truth, researches each one the way Ria would, scores whether they are even worth approaching, writes the first email, handles the reply and books the meeting. A new database next year is a new source, not a new project.

THE RESULT

Six demos a week instead of three.

Roughly 22 conversations a month, three to four new clients a month, 40 to 50 across the year. That is deliberately the conservative number. Omer's reaction on the call was that it is low — and I would rather be held to a floor we clear than a ceiling we miss.

03

What we're building

Six steps. Four of them happen on their own. Two of them are people — and both of those people are yours.

Following one facility all the way through

The clearest way to explain the system is to walk one nursing home through it, start to finish. This is a made-up facility, but every step is real and every term is one Ria used on our call.

  1. Monday, 6:00 a.m. — the system notices something newStep 1 · Watch

    Every morning the system checks the government's public inspection records. Overnight, a new report was published for Maple Grove Care Center, a 120-bed nursing home in Ohio.

    What is that report? When a nursing home is inspected, the government writes up everything it found wrong. Each finding gets a code — an "F-tag" — and a severity letter. That report becomes public about a month after the inspection. Nobody has to be tipped off. It is simply published, and almost nobody is watching for it.

  2. 6:01 a.m. — it reads what actually happenedStep 1 · Watch

    The system pulls the details out of the report: Maple Grove was cited under F-tag F607, which covers abuse. The severity was level J — the worst end of the scale, meaning actual harm or immediate jeopardy. They were fined $20,000 in civil monetary penalties across two survey cycles.

    These are exactly the signals Ria told us she looks for. Now they are attached to a record automatically, instead of being read off a screen by a person.

  3. 6:02 a.m. — it does Ria's homeworkStep 2 · Research

    Before writing anything, the system looks up the same things Ria looks up: the last three inspections — standard, complaint and quality-measure — whether the facility is on the government's special focus watch list, how big it is, who owns it, whether they already use a competitor, and whether they are even still in business.

    Why this matters: if the research shows they closed last month or already have a compliance partner, no email goes out at all. That check is the difference between a targeted system and a spam cannon.

  4. 6:03 a.m. — it writes the email in Ria's voiceStep 3 · Write

    Not a template with a name dropped in. The email names this facility's citation, this facility's penalty, and the specific ScribeRunner service that addresses it. Section 05 explains exactly how it learns to do this.

  5. 9:00 a.m. — Ria reads it before anyone else doesStep 4 · Check

    The draft waits in a simple approval queue. Ria opens it, reads it, and either approves it or fixes it. If she fixes it, the system learns from the correction and does not make that mistake again.

    Ria's own words on the call: "Once you send an email to a prospective client, you cannot realize later on that you made a mistake. From the get-go, the email has to be really good." That is why she is step four and not an afterthought.

  6. 9:05 a.m. — it sends, then it handles what comes backStep 5 · Send & reply

    The email goes out from a dedicated ScribeRunner address. When the administrator replies, the system reads the reply and sorts it: genuinely interested, asking a question, wrong person, not right now, or no.

    Interested replies get an answer the same day and a link to book time. Someone who says "call me after the new year" is filed and automatically comes back in January with everything they said the first time still attached. Anyone who says stop is never contacted again, anywhere.

  7. Meanwhile — everything is being written downThroughout · HubSpot

    None of the above lives in somebody's inbox. From the moment Maple Grove is picked up, HubSpot has a record: the facility as a company, the administrator as a contact, and the citation details on their own fields — F-tag, severity level, penalty amount, survey cycle, special-focus status.

    As things happen the record moves itself: found → researched → approved by Ria → emailed → replied → qualified → demo booked → won or lost. Every email sent and received is attached to it.

    This is what makes the reporting possible. Because every stage is stamped, you can later ask which severity levels convert best, which citations produce the fastest replies, and what a client from this channel actually cost you.

  8. Thursday, 2:00 p.m. — Juan takes the callStep 6 · Close

    Maple Grove's administrator is on the calendar. Waiting for Juan is a one-page brief: what they were cited for, what it cost them, what they have already said in email, and which ScribeRunner service to lead with.

    Juan closes it. Nothing in this system pitches, negotiates, or signs anybody. It gets the right conversation onto the right calendar with the right context, and then it gets out of the way.

→

Step 4 is a choice, not a permanent requirement

The walkthrough above shows Ria approving that email, and in the early months she approves every single one. That is on purpose, and it is temporary.

As the record builds — hundreds of drafts she approved without changing a word — the review moves from every email, to a sample, to only the unusual ones. By the end of month twelve the intent is that Ria can look if she wants to, not because anything is waiting on her. A citation gets published and a researched, written, personalised email goes out the same morning with no person in the loop at all.

She keeps the switch either way. Turning review back on for a new service line, a new facility type, or a message she has not seen before is a setting, not a rebuild.

The one open item, named plainly

I checked during our call: Star Pro does not publish a public API. An API is the normal way one piece of software hands data to another. Without one, there are two routes, and I would rather you hear both now than in week three.

Route one — ask Star Pro. Most software companies will provide developer access when a paying customer asks. This is the clean answer and it costs nothing but a phone call. I would want ScribeRunner to make that request in week one.

Route two — read the screen. If Star Pro declines, the system can log in and read the pages the way a person would, on a schedule. It works, and it is what I would build in the meantime. It is more fragile — if they redesign their site, it needs adjusting.

Either way this is not a blocker, because the CMS public records alone are enough to launch on. Star Pro makes the targeting sharper; it does not gate the project.

05

Teaching it to write like Ria

This is the part that decides whether the whole thing works. It is also the part that is genuinely hard, so here is exactly how it is done.

Omer asked the right question on our call: every agency on earth is blasting the same generic email, so how is this different? The answer is that the system does not get a template. It gets an apprenticeship.

"You had civil monetary penalties in survey cycle 1 and 2 in the amount of [X] due to a citation with scope and severity of level J under F-tag F607, which is abuse. Our company is designed with the goal of increasing regulatory compliance in your facility so that you avoid any civil monetary penalties."
Ria Ramos, writing a first-contact email out loud on our call · September 2026

That paragraph is the product. Everything in section 03 exists to put a sentence like that in front of the right administrator on the right morning. Here is how we get there.

  1. I sit with Ria and watch her workWeek 1 · ~2 hours

    Not a questionnaire. I screen-record while Ria picks a real facility, researches it, and writes the email she would actually send — and I ask why at every step. Why that facility. Why that citation and not the other one. Why lead with reimbursement instead of penalties.

    The "why" is the part that matters. Anyone can copy what Ria writes. The goal is to capture how she decides.

  2. We do it again, three to five timesWeek 1–2

    Different facility types, different citations, different severities. One with a level J abuse tag, one with a quality-measure problem, one that is on the special focus list, one that looks bad on paper but is not actually a fit.

    That last one is as valuable as the others — knowing who not to email is half of Ria's skill, and it is the half most systems never learn.

  3. I turn her method into instructions the system followsWeek 2–3

    Ria described her own process clearly on the call: identify the problem first, across the last three surveys. Then lay out which of our services solves it. Then state the value — fewer deficiencies, higher Medicare reimbursement, no penalties.

    That becomes the system's actual working method. It researches in that order and writes in that order, because that order is what works.

  4. We iterate until Ria stops correcting itWeek 3–4

    The first drafts will be wrong. I expect to go through thirteen, fourteen, fifteen rounds before they are good. Each round, Ria reads a batch and marks what is off — wrong tone, wrong service, a claim we cannot make, a detail misread.

    The gate is simple and it is hers: nothing goes to a real facility until Ria reads five in a row and says "that is what I would have said."

  5. The first fifty real emails are all reviewedMonth 2

    Even after the gate is passed, the first fifty emails to real facilities are read by a human before sending. Corrections keep feeding back in.

    After that we move to spot checks — a sample rather than every one — and only because the record by then shows it has earned it.

  6. Then we do the same thing with Juan and OmerMonth 3–4

    Omer suggested this himself: once Ria's clinical voice is captured, I shadow him and Juan for a few hours to learn the commercial side — how they qualify, what they ask, how they handle the objection that comes up every time.

    That is what teaches the system to handle replies rather than just write first emails.

What "the AI learns" actually means — and what it does not

"It learns" is used loosely in this industry, so here is the precise version. The system is not rewriting itself in the background. It has a written set of instructions — how to research, what to look for, how to phrase things, what never to claim. When Ria corrects a draft, that correction gets added to those instructions. The next batch reflects it.

That means the improvement is visible, reviewable, and reversible. You can read the instructions. You can see what changed and when. If a change makes things worse, it gets removed. Nothing about this is a black box, and nothing about it is out of your hands.

It also means the system is exactly as good as the time Ria puts in early, and honest about the trade: a few hours of her attention in month one is what buys hundreds of hours back later. If that time does not happen, this does not work, and no amount of technology substitutes for it.

Why the timing matters more than the message

A nursing home administrator who was cited three months ago has already been contacted by four vendors, has a plan of correction filed, and has moved on. An administrator who was cited last week is actively looking for help. Same facility, same email, completely different outcome — because of when it arrived.

There is also a second, quieter use for the same machinery. Ria pointed out that clinical audits are not only for facilities already in trouble — many buy them so they never get into trouble. The same watcher that spots a new citation can spot a facility whose quality measures are drifting the wrong way, and reach them before the inspector does. That is a different email, to a different mood, and it is the more profitable of the two conversations.

Every morning this also produces a short report: nothing new today, or five facilities were cited overnight, here is what each was cited for and here is which ones are worth your time. That report goes to whoever wants it, and it takes about eleven seconds to read.

%

What "60% automated" and "90% automated" actually mean

These numbers get thrown around loosely, so to be concrete. At the start, roughly 60–70% of the work happens without a person: finding facilities, researching them, drafting emails, sorting replies, scheduling follow-ups. The remaining 30–40% is Ria approving drafts and your team qualifying the people who reply.

By month twelve, that should be around 90% — meaning a lead can go from a published citation all the way to a booked demo with a prepared brief, and the first human to touch it is Juan on the call.

It will never be 100%, and I would not build it that way if you asked. The companies promising fully autonomous sales systems are the ones currently being cancelled by their customers. Something has to stay judgment, and that something is best kept close to the people whose name is on the email.
