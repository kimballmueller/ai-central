A Script Built to
Sell the Next Business

A seller testimonial isn't a highlight reel — it's your best salesperson. The real audience is the owner who hasn't called you yet: skeptical of brokers, scared of the process, wondering if their life's work is worth anything to anyone. Every scene below is designed to answer one of those doubts, in the seller's own words. That's what turns a video into listings, and listings into commissions.

The Premise

Three Jobs Every Video Has to Do

Before I write a single question, I know exactly what the footage needs to accomplish for your pipeline. Beautiful isn't the goal — beautiful is the baseline. These are the goals:

01
Win the listing

A seller trusts another seller more than any pitch deck. When a fence-sitting owner watches someone just like them say "I was skeptical too, and here's what actually happened," your listing call gets ten times easier.

02
De-risk the buyer

Buyers pay more and close faster when they can see the business, the operation, and a seller who has nothing to hide. B-roll of a real, running operation is due-diligence confidence you can't fake.

03
Feed the machine

Every interview is engineered so the answers cut cleanly into standalone shorts — ad creative, listing-page proof, outreach clips. One shoot day should feed your marketing for months, not one post.

The Script

Five-Beat Story Architecture

Every testimonial follows the same spine — a real narrative arc, not a string of compliments. Each beat has a job in your sales funnel, sample questions from the actual interview doc, and a note on what's on screen while it plays. Runtimes are for the 3–4 minute flagship cut.

1
The Cold Open 0:00 – 0:15
Job: stop the scroll

We open on the single most emotional line of the entire interview, pulled forward from wherever it happened. Not "Hi, I'm Dave from Dave's Dumpsters" — that's where testimonials go to die. Something like:

"I built this company for 31 years. I figured nobody could sell it but me — and I was wrong."

On screen: tight interview frame, then a hard cut to the best B-roll of the operation with the seller's name, business, and sale keyed over it.

2
The Life's Work 0:15 – 0:55
Job: earn trust through stakes

Who they are and what they built. This is short — but it can't be skipped, because the viewer needs to feel that this person had everything on the line. A testimonial only carries weight when the stakes are real.

"Take me back to the day you started this business — what did it look like then?" · "What did this company mean to your family?"

On screen: B-roll of the operation at work — hands, machines, crews, the sign out front. Old photos if the seller has them (I ask for these in pre-production, not on the day).

3
The Crossroads 0:55 – 1:40
Job: mirror the prospect's fear

Why sell — and what scared them about it. This is the beat most videographers cut, and it's the most valuable one you have. When your seller admits "I didn't trust brokers" or "I was afraid I'd get lowballed," the owner watching hears their own doubt said out loud — and then watches it get resolved. That's objection handling you didn't have to do on a call.

"What almost stopped you from selling?" · "Be honest — what did you think of business brokers before this?" · "What was the number you were afraid you'd hear?"

On screen: we slow down here — hold the interview frame, let the pauses breathe. Vulnerability doesn't need B-roll over it.

4
The BizDepot Chapter 1:40 – 2:50
Job: prove the process

The experience itself, walked through as specific moments rather than adjectives. "They were great to work with" is worthless on camera. "They called me back the same day the buyer flinched, and had it solved by Friday" is a closing tool. My questions are engineered to pull the specifics — valuation, finding the buyer, the negotiation, the moment it closed.

"Walk me through the moment you got the valuation — what did you expect, and what did they say?" · "Tell me about a moment in the deal where you thought it might fall apart." · "What surprised you most about how BizDepot handled it?"

On screen: interview intercut with operational B-roll. If the buyer is present, this is where their voice enters — the deal from the other side of the table.

5
The Other Side 2:50 – 3:45
Job: close the viewer

Life after the sale — and the seller's advice to the owner watching. This is your call to action, and it lands a hundred times harder because it doesn't come from your brand. It comes from someone who was standing exactly where the viewer is standing.

"What does a Tuesday look like for you now?" · "If an owner is watching this and they've been thinking about selling for two years but haven't made the call — what would you tell them?"

On screen: the warmest footage of the day — seller relaxed, with family or the buyer if present, handing over keys, walking the lot one last time. End card: BizDepot logo + one line + where to call.

When the Buyer Is There

The Handoff Cut

Same five-beat spine, but I interview seller and buyer separately, then weave them — the seller's fear in beat three answered by the buyer's excitement in beat four. The money shot is the two of them together in beat five: the handshake, the walkthrough, the "it's in good hands" moment. One extra hour of shooting, and it doubles the story: now the video sells owners on listing and shows buyers that BizDepot deals actually close.

The Craft

How the Questions Get Written

About 100 interviews in, I've learned the script is a floor, not a ceiling. Here's what goes into every question doc — and when I throw it out.

Every question targets an objection

Before the shoot I list the reasons a seller doesn't call BizDepot — don't trust brokers, afraid of a low number, worried about employees, "not ready yet." Every question in the doc exists to make the seller dismantle one of them on camera.

Answers built to stand alone

I coach every subject to fold the question into their answer — "the thing that surprised me most was…" — so each response cuts cleanly into a short with zero context needed. That's how one interview becomes fifteen pieces of ad creative.

Scripted structure, unscripted instincts

The doc keeps the story on its spine — but when a seller drops a thread the script never saw coming, I follow it, conversationally, and steer back when it's mined out. The best line of the video is almost never on the page. You only catch it if you know the structure well enough to leave it.

Comfort is engineered, not lucky

These are business owners, not actors. We start rolling on ten minutes of easy conversation that's never meant to be used — the business, the town, anything but the sale. By the time we hit the real questions, the camera has disappeared and I'm just a guy who's genuinely curious. Because I am.

Before I Ever Board a Plane

The Pre-Production System

The interview is won before it starts. This is the week leading into every shoot — it's why I walk in asking questions that sound like I've known the seller for years.

T-7
AI deep research

I run deep research on the seller, the business, the industry, and the local market — history, press, reviews, the competitive picture, what a sale like this means in that trade. This surfaces the story angles a generic question list never finds.

T-5
Human validation

AI gets things wrong, so I verify every fact I plan to reference before it goes anywhere near a question. Nothing kills an interview faster than "so tell me about your three locations" when they have two. Every detail in my doc is confirmed real.

T-3
Group text with the seller + BizDepot

A quick three-way thread to confirm a few details, set expectations for the day, and start the relationship before I'm standing in their shop with a camera. By shoot day I'm not a stranger — I'm the guy from the text thread. It also means zero surprises for your team.

DAY
Shoot day, solo

Roughly 45 minutes to a full two-camera cinematic interview setup — key light, backlight, clean audio, that Netflix-documentary look. Warm-up conversation, 60–90 minutes of interview, then B-roll of the operation keyed to the script beats. In and out without disrupting a business that's mid-transition — which the sellers, and the buyers, notice.
